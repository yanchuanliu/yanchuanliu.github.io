Research

International hierarchy, alliance politics, and the domestic constraints on foreign policy in the U.S.–China rivalry.

My research agenda explains China's tensions with democratic governments by integrating international structural pressures with domestic political dynamics. It develops along three streams: how international hierarchy shapes democratic alignment with U.S. China policy; the domestic determinants of security-oriented postures toward China across the democratic world; and the domestic constraints on China's own foreign policy ambitions.

Job market paper

The Electoral Logic of Foreign Policy Alignment: Security Dependence, Abandonment Risk, and Democratic Reactions to U.S.–China Competition

Under preparation for submission

As the leader of a hierarchical international order, Washington has frequently called on "like-minded" democratic partners to align their foreign policies with its own. During the strategic competition between the United States and China, why are some democratic states more receptive to Washington's demands for alignment than others? Existing international relations scholarship often treats states as unitary actors whose foreign policies respond to external threats. This perspective underestimates how hierarchical international relations shape domestic political dynamics surrounding foreign policy decisions. I argue instead that democratic responses to U.S.–China competition depend on two dimensions of a state's pre-existing security ties with the United States: dependence on U.S. security support and vulnerability to abandonment by the patron.

Through a most-similar comparison of Australia and New Zealand, two democracies similarly exposed to China but differently positioned within the U.S.-led hierarchy, I show that variation in security dependence and vulnerability to abandonment translates into divergent political processes at home. Australia's more dependent and more vulnerable position favors security-oriented parties by heightening public sensitivity to abandonment and incentivizing other parties to converge. By contrast, New Zealand's less dependent and less vulnerable position creates less pressure on both the public and the major parties to signal their intent to collaborate with the patron. The findings demonstrate that alignment is not an automatic response to external threats but a politically mediated process through which inter-state dynamics interact with domestic electoral incentives.

Dissertation

Ph.D. dissertation · UC San Diego

Between Dependence and Doubt: Democratic Responses to Great Power Competition

Committee: David A. Lake (Chair), Megumi Naoi, Stephan Haggard, Margaret E. Roberts, Victor Shih

My book-style dissertation explains variation in how advanced democracies have responded to strategic competition between the United States and China. It makes two theoretical contributions. First, against the conventional wisdom that states align in response to shifts in the distribution of power or an emerging threat, I argue that alignment decisions are politically motivated outcomes shaped by international hierarchy — specifically by security dependence and vulnerability to abandonment. Second, I combine insights from international relations and comparative politics to trace how domestic politics mediates that relationship: dependence and vulnerability together heighten public sensitivity to U.S. disengagement and empower elite actors with vested interests in the relationship, so that security-oriented actors gain leverage and accommodation-oriented ones face electoral pressure to converge.

I test the theory with a mixed-method design. Using ICEWS event data, I show that U.S.–China interactions predict Australia–China dynamics but not New Zealand–China dynamics. Cross-national survey analysis examines the effect of hierarchy on public opinion, and comparative case studies built on close readings of parliamentary debate trace its consequences for elite behavior.

Election Night Impacts: U.S. Leadership, Alliance Commitment, and Australian Preference for Policy Alignment

How do U.S. partners respond when Washington itself becomes a source of uncertainty rather than security? Existing theories offer contradictory predictions: subordinate partners may reinforce alignment to consolidate the relationship, or reduce it to hedge against disengagement. I adjudicate between them with an original survey experiment fielded among Australian voters on the eve of the 2024 U.S. presidential election, sponsored by the 21st Century China Center at UC San Diego. When U.S. credibility declines, Australian respondents are significantly less supportive of aligning Canberra's China policy with Washington's across three dimensions of great power competition. Heterogeneity analysis reveals an asymmetry: the backlash is driven primarily by non-conservative voters, while conservative support for alignment proves robust to the revival of an "America first" posture.

Balancing Engagement with Self-Reliance: Chinese Public Reactions to Economic Uncertainty

with Dapeng Cai

Does economic hardship fuel backlash against globalization outside advanced industrial societies? Using a survey experiment situated in the Chinese context, we find that public support for preserving ties with Western economies remains robust to signals of a struggling economy — challenging the conventional wisdom and pointing to a pattern potentially generalizable to other developing economies that have benefited from global integration.

Under review

National Distraction: Media Control during China's COVID Crisis

with Patrick Chester and Harris Doshay

Integrating theories of diversionary foreign policy with theories of autocratic censorship, we apply structural topic modeling to a corpus of news articles to test whether Chinese official media diverted attention from COVID-related deaths through foreign affairs coverage. A difference-in-differences design finds no evidence of diversion through foreign affairs, but shows that state media flooded the information environment with unrelated content — history, culture, entertainment. Foreign affairs is limited as a tool of distraction by availability: an autocratic government cannot always find a salient foreign event, and a minor one risks undermining regime legitimacy.

In progress

Anti-China Sentiment among Japanese Legislators

with Megumi Naoi

How does coalition politics shape elite threat perception? Preliminary field research, sponsored by the Joseph Naiman Graduate Fellowship, suggests that the Liberal Democratic Party's long-term partner, the Komeito, moderated the LDP's security-oriented positions until the coalition broke down in the 2025 elections. We draw on elite surveys, election records, and parliamentary speeches to investigate the relationship systematically.

In progress

Do As They Do? U.S. International Norm Violations and Chinese Public Opinion

with Samuel Liu and Brian O'Keefe

Will China seize the moment to rewrite international rules when the dominant power violates its own norms, or does China's stake in the existing order create incentives to uphold it? Rarely in history does a dominant power undo a hierarchical order from which it has benefited, leaving the debate inconclusive.
