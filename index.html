01 · About

I am a political scientist studying how democracies navigate great power competition. I hold a Ph.D. in Political Science from the University of California, San Diego and am currently a Research Associate at the China Institute, University of Alberta.

My work sits at the intersection of international relations and comparative politics. I use the tensions between China and the U.S.-led coalition of democracies as a lens on how states navigate hierarchy, alliances, and domestic constraints — and on how those same domestic constraints limit Beijing's own foreign policy ambitions.

My dissertation, Between Dependence and Doubt, asks why some advanced democracies converge with Washington's confrontational posture toward Beijing while others hold back. I argue that alignment is a politically motivated outcome shaped by international hierarchy: security dependence on the United States and vulnerability to American abandonment empower security-oriented actors, pressuring even accommodation-oriented ones to converge. I test this through a most-similar comparison of Australia and New Zealand, combining event data, cross-national survey analysis, and close readings of parliamentary debate.

Methodologically I work with survey experiments, large-N observational analysis, text analysis, and comparative case studies. My research appears in the Journal of Contemporary China and has been supported by the Institute for Humane Studies, the China Institute, and multiple units at UC San Diego.

Before graduate school I was a Research Fellow at the Harvard Kennedy School, a consultant at the World Bank Group, and a China-based political and economic analyst at the UK Foreign & Commonwealth Office.
